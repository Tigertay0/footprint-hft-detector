## 6. Methods and Results

### 6.1 Data

I tested the detector on real one-minute price and volume bars from Yahoo Finance, a free source any retail investor can use. Yahoo keeps one-minute bars for only about 30 days, so the study covers the 20 regular trading sessions from 21 September to 16 October 2026, from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. New York time. It uses the 20 tickers on the project's watchlist: six large technology stocks (TSLA, NVDA, AAPL, AMD, META, and AMZN), five index and leveraged funds (SPY, QQQ, TQQQ, SQQQ, and SOXL), and nine volatile stocks popular with retail traders (GME, AMC, PLTR, SOFI, COIN, MARA, RIVN, HOOD, and NIO). That is about 7,800 bars per ticker and 155,954 in total. Each bar holds an opening, high, low, and closing price and a volume, and nothing else: no individual orders, no trader identities, and no bid or ask prices. No artificial events were added to the data. Because Yahoo deletes one-minute bars after about a month, the raw bars were saved with the code so the results can be reproduced; both are available from the author.

### 6.2 Features

The model turns each minute into three numbers, each tied to one step of the momentum ignition pattern in Section 3.1. The volume Z-score measures how far the minute's trading volume, on a log scale, sits above the previous 20 minutes of the same session, in standard deviations; it captures bursts of rapid trading. The price reversion rate applies only when the price has made a sharp three-minute move, larger than 2.5 times its usual three-minute move over the past hour, and measures the share of that move given back over the next five minutes; it captures the push-and-reverse shape. The order imbalance proxy is a volume-weighted average, over five minutes, of where each bar closed inside its high-low range, from -1 for a close at the low to +1 for a close at the high. It estimates whether trading was one-sided, since minute bars do not show which side started each trade. All three features are computed within a single session, so the first 20 or so minutes of each day are not scored while the baselines fill.

### 6.3 Model and risk score

An Isolation Forest with 300 trees (Liu, Ting, and Zhou) was trained on the three features from the first 12 sessions (21 September to 6 October) and then scored the last eight (7 to 16 October), so it never saw the period it was judged on. Its contamination setting was 1 percent, which means it treats about the most unusual 1 percent of training minutes as anomalies. Each minute's anomaly score was converted to a percentile against the training minutes, and only minutes above the 95th percentile add to risk. Three rules based on the pattern in Section 5.2 run alongside the model: a volume Z-score above 2.5, more than half of a sharp move reversed, and an imbalance above 0.6 in either direction. The HFT risk score combines the two parts as 100 times the sum of 0.6 times the model's strength and 0.4 times the share of rules that fired. A score of 50 or more is Elevated and 70 or more is High. Alert minutes less than five minutes apart in the same session were merged into one episode, and each episode comes with a plain-English explanation of which signals fired.

Because the reversion signal needs five minutes of future prices, the tool also runs in a two-stage live mode. The moment a minute looks unusual, it sends an early warning based on volume, a sharp three-minute move, one-sided trading, and the model. Five minutes later it confirms the warning if the risk score is at least 50 and at least 35 percent of the move has reversed, or clears it if the move held. To measure this, each of the eight test sessions was replayed one minute at a time, exactly as a live data feed would deliver it.

### 6.4 What can and cannot be measured

No public list of manipulation events exists for these tickers and dates, so this study cannot report precision or recall. It measures what a retail user of the tool would experience instead: how often it alerts, which signals drive the alerts, and how many alerts are market-wide. An alert counted as market-wide when at least three of the other 19 tickers also had an alert episode within two minutes of it. A move that hits many unrelated stocks at the same moment is more likely to be news or a market-wide trade than manipulation of one stock.

### 6.5 Results

Across 58,206 scored test minutes, 1,043 (1.8 percent) reached Elevated or higher and 324 (0.6 percent) reached High. The Isolation Forest on its own flagged 536 minutes (0.9 percent), close to its 1 percent setting. Merging nearby minutes produced 788 alert episodes, or 4.9 per stock per trading day. The rate was similar across all 20 tickers, from 3.5 a day for MARA to 6.8 for NIO, so the large technology stocks alerted about as often as the volatile retail favorites. Most episodes were short, with a median length of one minute.

Most alerts did not have the momentum ignition shape. The volume rule fired in 74 percent of episodes and the one-sided trading rule in 62 percent, but the reversal rule fired in only 15 percent (122 episodes). Most of what the model flagged was a burst of heavy, one-directional trading whose price move then held.

Many alerts were market-wide. In 221 of the 788 episodes (28 percent), three or more other tickers alerted within two minutes, and on two occasions 9 of the 20 tickers alerted in the same minute (12:17 p.m. on 8 October and 12:00 p.m. on 9 October). Only 199 episodes (25 percent) had no other ticker alerting within two minutes. Episodes with a reversal were more often market-wide than the rest (48 percent against 25 percent). Removing market-wide episodes and keeping only those with a reversal leaves 64 episodes, about 0.4 per stock per day. These stock-specific push-and-reverse episodes are the closest match to momentum ignition in this data, but without labels they remain candidates, not confirmed cases.

The live replay sent 646 early warnings, about four per stock per day. Five minutes later, 586 of them (91 percent) were cleared because the move held, and 41 (6 percent) were confirmed as reversals; the rest came too close to the end of a session to resolve. Half of the confirmed alerts (21 of 41) were market-wide, compared with about a fifth of early warnings (138 of 646), so many of the reversals the live mode confirmed were moves that hit several stocks at once. That leaves 20 stock-specific confirmed alerts, about one per stock every eight trading days. The full score also raised 167 alerts that had not been preceded by an early warning.

These results answer the paper's second question only in part. The detector runs on free data and explains its alerts as they happen, but at its default setting it alerts about five times per stock per day, and most of those alerts describe heavy trading that did not reverse. A retail investor would need the alerts filtered: dropping market-wide episodes removes 28 percent of them, and also requiring the reversal pattern brings the rate down to about one alert per stock every two to three trading days. The study has clear limits. It covers only 20 tickers and eight test sessions in a single month. Without labeled cases, it cannot say how many alerts were manipulation or how many real events it missed. The imbalance feature is an estimate from the shape of each bar, not real order flow. And the busiest part of each day, the first 20 minutes after the open, was not scored because the baselines were still filling.
