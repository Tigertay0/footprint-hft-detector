Adetayo Agueh

High-frequency trading (HFT) is estimated to make up around half of U.S. equity volume, and a small share of that activity crosses into manipulation. This paper reviews research and enforcement records on how HFT manipulation works, who pays for it, and how it is detected, then tests whether a detector built on free data could serve retail investors. The review finds five recurring strategies. Momentum ignition and expiry-day index manipulation leave patterns long enough to appear in public minute-level data, while spoofing and quote stuffing do not, and most published detection methods depend on order-level data or labeled cases that retail investors cannot access. I built a detector that scores each minute with an Isolation Forest and three rules (volume bursts, price reversals, and one-sided trading) and ran it, with no planted events, on real Yahoo Finance one-minute bars for 20 U.S. stocks and funds from 21 September to 16 October 2026. Over eight test sessions it raised 788 alert episodes, about five per stock per day. Only 15 percent had the push-and-reverse shape of momentum ignition, 28 percent coincided with alerts in three or more other tickers, and in a live replay 91 percent of early warnings were cleared within five minutes because the move held. Keeping only stock-specific reversals left about 0.4 alerts per stock per day. No labeled manipulation exists for this period, so precision could not be measured. A free-data tool can flag unusual trading as it happens, but it needs market-wide and reversal filters before its alerts are useful to a retail investor, and it cannot prove manipulation.

## 1. Introduction

Between January 2023 and March 2025, one trading firm allegedly pushed India's Bank Nifty index up in the morning and down later in the day on the days its options expired. According to India's market regulator, SEBI, the Jane Street group did this on 15 of the 18 Bank Nifty expiry days it examined. On those days the firm bought large amounts of Bank Nifty stocks and futures in the morning, often at or above the last traded price, while holding options positions that would pay off if the index fell. On SEBI's example day, 17 January 2024, the buying ran until 11:47 a.m., and from 11:49 a.m. the firm sold almost all of it (SEBI, Interim Order). In July 2025, SEBI barred the firm from Indian markets and ordered it to deposit ₹4,843.57 crore, about $567 million at the time, as alleged unlawful gains (SEBI, Interim Order). Jane Street paid the money into escrow, and the ban was lifted on 21 July 2025 (SEBI, "Update"). The firm denies manipulation and has called its trades index arbitrage ("Jane Street to Challenge"). The order is still interim: in October 2026, Jane Street was contesting it at the Securities Appellate Tribunal, and SEBI had not issued a final ruling ("Jane Street Accuses"). The same regulator found that 93 percent of individual traders in Indian equity derivatives lost money in fiscal years 2022 through 2024, with combined losses above ₹1.8 lakh crore (SEBI, "Updated SEBI Study"), and that 87.7 percent still lost money in fiscal year 2026 (SEBI, Profitability). The case is an example of the problem this paper studies: a strategy run by algorithms on public markets, against traders who have no way of seeing it.

High-frequency trading (HFT) uses computer programs to execute orders in microseconds or milliseconds. Estimates of its share of U.S. equity trading volume have typically been around half, though they depend on how HFT is defined (SEC, Concept Release; SEC Staff), and public data does not show when it is happening (Ibikunle et al.). It is run by trading firms such as Citadel Securities, Virtu Financial, Jane Street, and Hudson River Trading (Citadel Securities; Global Trading; IFLR). These firms rent space for their servers inside exchange data centers, a practice called co-location, and buy direct data feeds that deliver prices faster than the public consolidated feed most investors see (Adrian). HFT strategies in general fall into a few groups: market making, which profits from the gap between buy and sell prices; latency arbitrage, which trades on price changes before slower exchanges update; and illegal manipulative strategies such as momentum ignition, which triggers a sharp price move, lets other traders follow it, then reverses (Menkveld; Budish, Cramton, and Shim; SEC, Concept Release). Small traders appear to pay more than large ones: in E-mini S&P 500 futures in 2010, aggressive HFTs earned $3.49 per contract trading against small traders, compared with $1.92 against large fundamental traders (Baron, Brogaard, and Kirilenko). Algorithmic trading, including HFT market making, has also narrowed bid-ask spreads, which lowers trading costs for most investors (Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld; SEC Staff), but Ibikunle et al. find that HFT that demands liquidity goes with slower price discovery around earnings announcements.

Regulators and researchers have worked on this problem for more than a decade. The SEC built the Consolidated Audit Trail so that regulators can track every order in U.S. listed stocks and options (SEC, "Rule 613"), and enforcement actions have followed, from the SEC's first HFT manipulation case, against Athena Capital Research in 2014, to the first criminal spoofing conviction in 2015 (SEC, "SEC Charges"; U.S. Attorney's Office). Academic researchers have described the patterns manipulation leaves in the data and have trained machine learning models to find them (Tse, Lin, and Vincent; Golmohammadi, Zaiane, and Díaz; Cao et al.; Alfajeer et al.).

Most of this work is built for someone other than the retail investor. The Consolidated Audit Trail is open only to the SEC and the self-regulatory organizations, such as the exchanges and FINRA. Most academic models are trained on tick-level order book data or confidential trade records that cost thousands of dollars or cannot be bought at all. Enforcement arrives years after the trading: Athena's manipulation took place in 2009 and was charged in 2014 (SEC, "SEC Charges"). A retail investor deciding whether to buy a stock this afternoon has nothing that tells them whether its price is being pushed.

This paper asks two questions. First, what does existing research show about how HFT manipulation works and how much it costs retail investors? Second, can the detection methods that research describes be adapted to the minute-level price and volume data that a retail investor can get for free? Section 2 explains how legitimate HFT strategies make money and where manipulation begins. Section 3 reviews five manipulative strategies. Section 4 weighs the evidence on retail harm against the evidence on benefits. Section 5 reviews detection methods, from regulatory surveillance to machine learning. Section 6 presents the methods and results. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. How HFT Makes Money, and Where Manipulation Begins

Most HFT activity is legal, and some of it helps other investors. In market making, a firm posts a buy order and a sell order at the same time, for example a bid at $100.00 and an offer at $100.05, and earns the five-cent gap plus a small rebate from the exchange when its orders fill (Menkveld). The risk is adverse selection: a better-informed trader can hit the firm's quote just before the price moves against it. Brogaard, Hendershott, and Riordan studied NASDAQ data on HFT trades and found that HFTs, overall, trade in the direction of permanent price changes and against temporary ones, which means they help prices reach their correct level faster.

Latency arbitrage is harder to defend. When a price changes on one exchange, a firm with faster connections can trade on another exchange before that exchange's quote updates. The profit comes from information other traders have not received yet. Budish, Cramton, and Shim argue that this is a flaw in market design. Because U.S. exchanges process orders one at a time in a continuous stream, a firm that is even a microsecond faster can always pick off stale quotes, so firms keep spending money on speed that does nothing for anyone else. Their proposed fix is to run auctions in short batches, so that speed differences smaller than the batch interval stop mattering. Aquilina, Budish, and O'Neill later measured these races directly, using London Stock Exchange message data that records failed attempts to trade as well as successful ones. For FTSE 100 stocks, races happened about once per minute per stock, the typical race was decided in 5 to 10 millionths of a second, and races made up about 20 percent of trading volume. Six firms accounted for over 80 percent of race wins and losses. The authors estimate that latency arbitrage works like a tax of about 0.5 basis points on trading, worth around $5 billion a year in global equity markets. Shkilko and Sokolov found a related effect in the United States: when bad weather disrupted the microwave networks that the fastest traders use, adverse selection and trading costs fell.

Ibikunle et al. show that the HFT population splits along this line. Using machine learning measures of HFT activity for U.S. stocks from 2010 to 2023, they find that HFT that supplies liquidity goes with faster price discovery around earnings announcements, while HFT that demands liquidity goes with slower price discovery.

Manipulation is a narrower category. Putniņš defines market manipulation as trading or order placement meant to move a price away from where supply and demand would put it, so that the manipulator can profit from the distortion. Among the types he describes are trade-based manipulation, which uses real trades, and order-based manipulation, which uses orders the trader never intends to fill. Both types appear in HFT. What makes HFT manipulation different from older forms, such as pump-and-dump schemes, is its scale and speed: a single algorithm can repeat the same pattern hundreds of times a day across many stocks, and each episode can be over in seconds.

## 3. Five Manipulative Strategies

### 3.1 Momentum ignition

The SEC described momentum ignition in 2010, in a review of U.S. equity market structure, as a strategy in which a trader places a series of orders and trades meant to set off a rapid price move up or down, then profits from that move (SEC, Concept Release). Analysts at Credit Suisse later built a detector for it. In the pattern Tse, Lin, and Vincent describe, the price holds steady while volume spikes, then the price makes a large move on high volume, and then it reverts on low volume. Other algorithms and human traders see the move and follow it. The manipulator then reverses, selling into the buying it caused, or buying back into the selling. In European STOXX 600 stocks in the third quarter of 2012, they counted an average of 1.6 such events per stock per day, with an average price move of 38 basis points lasting about 1.5 minutes. They note that their detector cannot show intent, so these are possible cases, not proven ones. An event that lasts a minute and a half leaves a mark in minute-level price and volume data, which is the data a retail investor can see, though at one-minute resolution it covers only one or two bars. A similar shape appears in a recent U.S. case that did not involve an HFT firm. In April 2026, the SEC charged a trader with making over $5 million between 2021 and 2024 in more than 1,000 trading sessions, in which he allegedly pushed prices up with many small buy orders, placed buy orders he did not intend to fill, sold into the rise, and cancelled the orders (SEC, "Harsh V. Patel").

### 3.2 Spoofing and layering

A spoofer places large orders on one side of the order book with no intention of letting them fill. The orders make it look as if demand or supply is building, other traders adjust their prices, and the spoofer trades on the other side at the better price before cancelling the fake orders. Layering is the same idea with several orders stacked at different prices. Lee, Eom, and Park studied spoofing traders in the Korean stock market and found that the strategy was profitable and that spoofers chose stocks and times where their fake orders would move prices most. In the United States, Michael Coscia used an algorithm in 2011 to place and cancel orders in gold, soybean meal, soybean oil, copper, and euro and British pound futures, making nearly $1.4 million in under three months. In 2015 he became the first person convicted under the anti-spoofing provision of the Dodd-Frank Act, and he was sentenced to three years in prison (U.S. Attorney's Office). A federal appeals court upheld the conviction in 2017 (United States v. Coscia). Spoofing cases have since grown much larger: in 2020, JPMorgan agreed to pay more than $920 million over spoofing by its precious metals and Treasury traders, although the traders in that case were people, not algorithms (U.S. Department of Justice). The fake orders are cancelled before they fill, so they never appear in trade prices or volume, only in order book data. Current spoofing detectors, such as the real-time monitor Tao et al. built from order book imbalance, depend on that data.

### 3.3 Quote stuffing

In quote stuffing, an algorithm floods the market with orders and cancellations to slow down the data feed that other traders depend on. The flood creates delays that the stuffer, who knows its own orders are noise, can exploit. Egginton, Van Ness, and Van Ness found at least one quote-stuffing episode in more than 74 percent of U.S. exchange-listed securities in 2010, and found that during intense quoting episodes, liquidity fell while trading costs and short-term volatility rose. Like spoofing, quote stuffing happens in orders and cancellations, so it does not show up in minute-level price and volume data.

### 3.4 Marking the close

Many investors, index funds, and derivatives use a stock's closing price, so moving that one price can be worth a lot. From June to December 2009, Athena Capital Research ran an algorithm its employees called "Gravy," which placed large numbers of trades in the last two seconds of almost every NASDAQ trading day to push closing prices in the direction that benefited its positions. In the affected stocks, Athena's trades made up more than 70 percent of NASDAQ volume in the seconds before the close. In 2014 the SEC brought what it called its first high-frequency trading manipulation case, and Athena settled, paying a $1 million penalty without admitting or denying the findings. The SEC's enforcement director, Andrew Ceresney, said that traders can use complex algorithms, "but what happened here was fraud" (SEC, "SEC Charges"). The practice is probably far more common than enforcement records show: Comerton-Forde and Putniņš estimate that about 1 percent of closing prices are manipulated, and that only a small fraction of these cases are detected and prosecuted.

### 3.5 Expiry-day index manipulation

The Jane Street case described in the introduction combines several of these ideas across a full trading day. According to SEBI's interim order, the morning buying pushed the Bank Nifty index up. For 17 January 2024, SEBI calculated that Jane Street's trades pushed the index up by about as much as all other participants combined pushed it down (SEBI, Interim Order). The later selling pushed the index down on the day options expired, when the firm's options positions were most sensitive to the final price. The alleged profit came from the options; the stock and futures trades could lose money on their own. On six other expiry days, SEBI alleges a second pattern, which it calls "extended marking the close," aimed at the index's closing level. This structure is harder to catch than momentum ignition because the manipulative trades and the profitable trades are in different markets. Chang et al. document a similar cross-market pattern in Taiwan, where traders whose options positions gained from a higher index bought the largest index stocks during the settlement window on expiration days, and traders positioned for a lower index sold them. SEBI has since tightened the rules around expiry days. From November 2024, each exchange could offer only one weekly index expiry, contracts became larger, and expiry-day margins rose, and from April 2025, position limits have been monitored during the trading day as well as at the close (SEBI, "Measures"). According to press reports, India added a closing auction session in August 2026 and plans to settle index derivatives on a 30-minute average price from December 2026 (PTI).

## 4. Who Pays: Evidence on Retail Harm and Benefit

The most direct measurement of who loses to HFT comes from Baron, Brogaard, and Kirilenko, who used confidential regulatory data that tags the trading account on both sides of every trade in the E-mini S&P 500 futures contract, and sorted accounts into types by how they traded. HFTs earned more than $23 million in gross trading profits in that contract in August 2010 alone. Aggressive HFTs, the ones that take liquidity rather than provide it, earned $3.49 per contract when trading against small traders and $1.92 per contract against large fundamental traders, on a contract worth about $50,000. Small traders therefore paid about 82 percent more per contract than fundamental traders. These are short-term profits, measured over a minute or less, which the authors read as a trading cost for the other side. The numbers come from a 2012 working paper; the published version of the study uses Swedish stock data instead and does not report them (Baron et al.).

The harm is not spread evenly across retail investors. A Congressional Research Service report, summarizing a Canadian study by Malinova, Park, and Riordan, noted that retail investors who day trade may be more affected by HFT, while buy-and-hold investors who trade rarely are less likely to be affected (Shorter and Miller). The strategies in Section 3 explain why. A momentum ignition event costs something only to the trader who buys during the artificial rise, and a person who buys an index fund once a month is rarely that trader. An active trader following short-term price moves is exactly the trader momentum ignition is designed to attract.

Speed differences add to the cost. Jian, Yin, and Li built an agent-based simulation of the Chinese market, using order book data to set up the simulated environment, in which the chance that a retail order is intercepted through latency arbitrage rose to over 82 percent once the gap in communication delay between retail traders and HFTs passed 50 milliseconds. The 50-millisecond threshold is part of the model's assumptions, and this is a simulation, not a measurement of real losses, but it shows how quickly the disadvantage can grow once the speed gap passes a threshold. The one large measurement from real trading data puts the cost per trade much lower: half a basis point is about 5 cents on a $1,000 trade (Aquilina, Budish, and O'Neill).

HFT can also make market-wide stress worse. Kirilenko et al. studied the Flash Crash of May 6, 2010, when U.S. stock prices collapsed and recovered within minutes. They found that HFTs did not trigger the crash but that their trading, in response to an unusually large sell order, made the volatility worse.

Other parts of market structure also shape what retail investors pay. Schwarz et al. placed 85,000 simultaneous market orders through six accounts at five U.S. brokers and found that average round-trip costs ranged from 0.07 to 0.46 percent, because the wholesalers that execute retail orders gave different brokers different prices for the same trades. In U.S. options, retail traders now account for over 60 percent of volume; they favor cheap weekly contracts with an average bid-ask spread of 12.6 percent, and they lose money on average (Bryzgalova, Pavlova, and Sikorskaya).

Against this evidence, HFT competition has lowered the direct cost of trading. Algorithmic trading, including HFT market making, has narrowed bid-ask spreads (Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld; SEC Staff), and liquidity-supplying HFTs make prices more accurate (Brogaard, Hendershott, and Riordan; Ibikunle et al.). The two bodies of evidence are compatible. Every retail trade may be slightly cheaper because of HFT market making, while the retail traders who trade most often, and at the worst moments, lose more to aggressive and manipulative strategies. Averages across all investors hide the second effect. The problem this paper addresses is the second group.

## 5. How Manipulation Is Detected Today

### 5.1 Regulatory surveillance

Regulators have the best data. The SEC adopted Rule 613 in 2012 and approved the plan for the Consolidated Audit Trail in 2016, creating a central database that lets regulators track all activity in U.S. listed stocks and options, including which firm placed each order (SEC, "Rule 613"). With that data, a regulator can see a spoofing pattern directly: the same firm placing and cancelling large orders just before trading on the other side. The system is now being scaled back. A federal appeals court struck down the CAT's funding order in July 2025 (American Securities Association v. SEC), and the SEC has since stopped CAT reporting of individual customers' personal information. In August 2026, SEC Chairman Paul Atkins asked staff to draft a rule that would rescind Rule 613 and have firms report the same trading data to the SEC or its designee, a change he expected to take until late 2027 (Atkins). For retail investors, the limits are access and timing. The data is available only to the SEC and the self-regulatory organizations, and enforcement takes years. Athena's 2009 trading was charged in 2014, Coscia's 2011 trading ended in a 2015 conviction, and SEBI's interim order against Jane Street came almost a year after the last of the 18 Bank Nifty expiry days it examined, with no final ruling by October 2026 (SEC, "SEC Charges"; U.S. Attorney's Office; SEBI, Interim Order; "Jane Street Accuses"). Enforcement punishes manipulation after the fact and gives no warning to the investor who is trading against it.

### 5.2 Rule-based detection

The simplest detection methods write the known pattern as a set of rules. Tse, Lin, and Vincent describe momentum ignition as a sequence: a volume spike while the price holds steady, a large price move on high volume, and a reversal on low volume. A rule-based detector flags any window where all three conditions are met. Rules are easy to explain, which matters for a non-expert user, but they only find the patterns someone has already described, and a manipulator who changes one step of the sequence can avoid them.

### 5.3 Supervised machine learning

Supervised models learn from examples labeled as manipulated or not manipulated. Golmohammadi, Zaiane, and Díaz used eight stocks named in SEC manipulation cases filed in 2003 as their positive examples and tested eight algorithms, including random forests, neural networks, and support vector machines, on hourly trading data from 64 companies. Naive Bayes performed best, catching 89 percent of manipulated observations with 83 percent specificity. Do and Putniņš trained models on a global sample of prosecuted spoofing and layering cases and reached high out-of-sample accuracy, but their strongest signals, such as lopsided quotes and unusual cancellation rates, come from order-level data. The weakness is the labels. Confirmed manipulation cases are rare, they come from enforcement actions years after the trading, and they include only the cases regulators caught. A model trained on them learns what caught manipulation looks like. A 2025 review of 66 detection studies found the same gaps across the field: models are often tested on synthetic data, and few work in near real time (Alfajeer et al.).

### 5.4 Unsupervised machine learning

Unsupervised models do not need labels. They learn what normal trading looks like and flag what does not fit. Cao et al. built an adaptive hidden Markov model with extra "anomaly states" for price manipulation and tested it on real tick data from seven NASDAQ and London Stock Exchange stocks into which they inserted synthetic manipulation patterns; it outperformed the benchmark methods it was compared with. Poutré, Chételat, and Morales trained a transformer autoencoder on NASDAQ order book data and used it to find injected quote stuffing, layering, and pump-and-dump patterns. Isolation Forest, introduced by Liu, Ting, and Zhou, takes a different approach. It builds many random decision trees that split the data on random features at random values. Unusual points get separated from the rest in fewer splits than normal points, so the average number of splits needed to isolate a point becomes its anomaly score. The method is fast, needs no labels, and works with a small number of features, which makes it a good fit for data with few columns.

Recent studies have tested unsupervised models on data closer to what a retail investor has. Núñez Delafuente, Astudillo, and Díaz applied Isolation Forests to hourly price, return, volume, and trade-count features for eight stocks named in SEC cases. A single forest found about two-thirds of the manipulated hours, and an ensemble of forests found up to 89 percent, but only 2 to 6 percent of the hours they flagged were actually manipulated. In a 2026 preprint, Chen and Hybinette ran minute-level detectors on public Bank Nifty options data covering 209 trading days in 2024, 10 of which SEBI identified in the Jane Street case. An autoencoder flagged all 10 and an Isolation Forest flagged 8, but about three of every four flagged days had no known manipulation, and the two marking-the-close days were the hardest to catch. Some of those flagged days may be manipulation that was never prosecuted, since few cases are caught (Comerton-Forde and Putniņš). Legitimate trading can also produce similar shapes: dealers hedging same-day options can strengthen intraday reversals and momentum on their own (Dim, Eraker, and Vilkov).

### 5.5 Measuring HFT from public data

The study closest to the retail problem is Ibikunle et al. They trained machine learning models on a Nasdaq dataset of 120 stocks from 2008 and 2009 in which HFT activity is identified, then applied those models to public intraday data to estimate HFT activity for more than 8,000 U.S. stocks from 2010 to 2023. Their measures performed better than older proxies and moved in the expected direction around known events, such as exchange speed bumps and data feed upgrades. Their goal was measurement, not manipulation detection, but they show that public data carries enough information to estimate HFT activity when a model is trained properly.

## 6. Methods and Results

### 6.1 Data

I tested the detector on real one-minute price and volume bars from Yahoo Finance, a free source any retail investor can use. Yahoo keeps one-minute bars for only about 30 days, so the study covers the 20 regular trading sessions from 21 September to 16 October 2026, from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. New York time. It uses the 20 tickers on the project's watchlist: six large technology stocks (TSLA, NVDA, AAPL, AMD, META, and AMZN), five index and leveraged funds (SPY, QQQ, TQQQ, SQQQ, and SOXL), and nine volatile stocks popular with retail traders (GME, AMC, PLTR, SOFI, COIN, MARA, RIVN, HOOD, and NIO). That is about 7,800 bars per ticker and 155,954 in total. Each bar holds an opening, high, low, and closing price and a volume, and nothing else: no individual orders, no trader identities, and no bid or ask prices. No artificial events were added to the data. Because Yahoo deletes one-minute bars after about a month, the raw bars were saved with the code so the results can be reproduced; both are available from the author.

### 6.2 Features

The model turns each minute into three numbers, each tied to one step of the momentum ignition pattern in Section 3.1. The volume Z-score measures how far the minute's trading volume, on a log scale, sits above the previous 20 minutes of the same session, in standard deviations; it captures bursts of rapid trading. The price reversion rate applies only when the price has made a sharp three-minute move, larger than 2.5 times its usual three-minute move over the past hour, and measures the share of that move given back over the next five minutes; it captures the push-and-reverse shape. The order imbalance proxy is a volume-weighted average, over five minutes, of where each bar closed inside its high-low range, from -1 for a close at the low to +1 for a close at the high. It estimates whether trading was one-sided, since minute bars do not show which side started each trade. All three features are computed within a single session, so the first 20 or so minutes of each day are not scored while the baselines fill.

### 6.3 Model and risk score

An Isolation Forest with 300 trees (Liu, Ting, and Zhou) was trained on the three features from the first 12 sessions (21 September to 6 October) and then scored the last eight (7 to 16 October), so it never saw the period it was judged on. Its contamination setting was 1 percent, which means it treats about the most unusual 1 percent of training minutes as anomalies. Each minute's anomaly score was converted to a percentile against the training minutes, and only minutes above the 95th percentile add to risk. Three rules based on the pattern in Section 5.2 run alongside the model: a volume Z-score above 2.5, more than half of a sharp move reversed, and an imbalance above 0.6 in either direction. The HFT risk score combines the two parts as 100 times the sum of 0.6 times the model's strength and 0.4 times the share of rules that fired. A score of 50 or more is Elevated and 70 or more is High. Alert minutes less than five minutes apart in the same session were merged into one episode, and each episode comes with a plain-English explanation of which signals fired.

Because the reversion signal needs five minutes of future prices, the tool also runs in a two-stage live mode. The moment a minute looks unusual, it sends an early warning based on volume, a sharp three-minute move, one-sided trading, and the model. Five minutes later it confirms the warning if the risk score is at least 50 and at least 35 percent of the move has reversed, or clears it if the move held. To measure this, each of the eight test sessions was replayed one minute at a time, exactly as a live data feed would deliver it.

### 6.4 What can and cannot be measured

No public list of manipulation events exists for these tickers and dates, so this study cannot report precision or recall. It measures what a retail user of the tool would experience instead: how often it alerts, which signals drive the alerts, and how many alerts are market-wide. An alert counted as market-wide when at least three of the other 19 tickers also had an alert episode within two minutes of it. A move that hits many unrelated stocks at the same moment is more likely to be news or a market-wide trade than manipulation of one stock.

### 6.5 Results

Across 58,206 scored test minutes, 1,043 (1.8 percent) reached Elevated or higher and 324 (0.6 percent) reached High. The Isolation Forest on its own flagged 536 minutes (0.9 percent), close to its 1 percent setting. Merging nearby minutes produced 788 alert episodes, or 4.9 per stock per trading day. The rate was similar across all 20 tickers, from 3.5 a day for MARA to 6.8 for NIO, so the large technology stocks alerted about as often as the volatile retail favorites. Most episodes were short, with a median length of one minute.

Most alerts did not have the momentum ignition shape. The volume rule fired in 74 percent of episodes and the one-sided trading rule in 62 percent, but the reversal rule fired in only 15 percent (122 episodes). Most of what the model flagged was a burst of heavy, one-directional trading whose price move then held.

Many alerts were market-wide. In 221 of the 788 episodes (28 percent), three or more other tickers alerted within two minutes, and on two occasions 9 of the 20 tickers alerted in the same minute (12:17 p.m. on 8 October and 12:00 p.m. on 9 October). Only 199 episodes (25 percent) had no other ticker alerting within two minutes. Episodes with a reversal were more often market-wide than the rest (48 percent against 25 percent). Removing market-wide episodes and keeping only those with a reversal leaves 64 episodes, about 0.4 per stock per day. These stock-specific push-and-reverse episodes are the closest match to momentum ignition in this data, but without labels they remain candidates, not confirmed cases.

The live replay sent 646 early warnings, about four per stock per day. Five minutes later, 586 of them (91 percent) were cleared because the move held, and 41 (6 percent) were confirmed as reversals; the rest came too close to the end of a session to resolve. Half of the confirmed alerts (21 of 41) were market-wide, compared with about a fifth of early warnings (138 of 646), so many of the reversals the live mode confirmed were moves that hit several stocks at once. That leaves 20 stock-specific confirmed alerts, about one per stock every eight trading days. The full score also raised 167 alerts that had not been preceded by an early warning.

These results answer the paper's second question only in part. The detector runs on free data and explains its alerts as they happen, but at its default setting it alerts about five times per stock per day, and most of those alerts describe heavy trading that did not reverse. A retail investor would need the alerts filtered: dropping market-wide episodes removes 28 percent of them, and also requiring the reversal pattern brings the rate down to about one alert per stock every two to three trading days. The study has clear limits. It covers only 20 tickers and eight test sessions in a single month. Without labeled cases, it cannot say how many alerts were manipulation or how many real events it missed. The imbalance feature is an estimate from the shape of each bar, not real order flow. And the busiest part of each day, the first 20 minutes after the open, was not scored because the baselines were still filling.

## 7. Conclusion

HFT manipulation takes at least five forms, from momentum ignition to expiry-day index manipulation, and the evidence suggests that small, active traders pay more for it than institutions do. Most tools built to catch it serve regulators and institutions: they need order-level data retail investors cannot access, and they work years after the trading. Research on public data, especially the work of Ibikunle et al. and recent tests of unsupervised detectors, shows that this gap can be narrowed. Momentum ignition and expiry-day index manipulation last long enough to appear in free minute-level data, while spoofing and quote stuffing do not. The detector tested here, an Isolation Forest paired with three rules, runs on free one-minute data and explains each alert in plain English. On 20 real tickers, though, it alerted about five times per stock per day, and most alerts were heavy one-sided trading or market-wide moves, not the push-and-reverse pattern of momentum ignition. Dropping market-wide alerts and keeping only reversals brought the rate down to about one alert per stock every two to three trading days. The next step is to test those filtered alerts against labeled cases, such as the dates in SEBI's Jane Street order or SEC litigation releases, using historical minute data from a broker or data vendor. Such a tool could not prove manipulation. It could tell a retail investor, before they trade, that the price they are about to chase may be artificial.
